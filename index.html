What is a BDC?

A Business Development Company (BDC) is a closed-end investment company registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940 that provides financing to small and mid-sized private companies. BDCs were created by Congress in 1980 to incentivise capital flow to businesses that lack access to public markets.

Key Regulatory Requirements

  • Must invest at least 70% of assets in “qualifying assets” (private US companies with <$250M market cap)
  • Maximum leverage: 2:1 debt-to-equity (increased from 1:1 by the Small Business Credit Availability Act 2018)
  • Must distribute at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders (RIC status, no corporate tax)
  • Must offer “managerial assistance” to portfolio companies
  • Publicly traded on stock exchanges with daily liquidity (unlike private credit funds)

External vs. Internal Management

Most BDCs are externally managed: a parent asset manager (e.g. Blue Owl, Ares, Blackstone) runs the BDC through an investment advisory agreement. The manager earns a base management fee (typically 1.0–1.75% of assets) plus an incentive fee (typically 15–20% of income above a hurdle rate). A small number of BDCs (e.g. Main Street Capital) are internally managed, with no external adviser.

Detailed information only available within 10-K

Data on the underlying portfolio companies is available in XBRL files; however, these files capture only approximately 60–70% of the holding-level information contained in a 10-K filing. This missing information is particularly important for credit quality assessment and concentration risk analysis.

The XBRL data provides the quantitative foundation of the portfolio, what assets are held, their cost basis, and their fair value. However, the qualitative context, including why investments were made, how they are managed, and the associated risks, is available only in the accompanying 10-K.

Key information that is not captured in the XBRL data includes:

  • Geographic exposure at the individual holding level (only portfolio-level concentration percentages are provided).
  • Borrower or portfolio company business descriptions.
  • Sector classifications for individual holdings, as sector labels are assigned only to subtotal rows rather than each investment.
  • Maturity dates for most investments, with this field populated for only around 20% of holdings.
